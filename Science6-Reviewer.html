Living things and cycles of matter

Biotic and abiotic factors

  • Biotic factors are the living things in an ecosystem: plants, animals, fungi, and bacteria.
  • Abiotic factors are the non-living things: sunlight, water, air, soil, rocks, and temperature.
  • Dead leaves and logs are biotic because they were once living.

Interactions can bring good or harm

Brings goodBrings harm
Sunlight, water, and soil help plants grow. A bee gets nectar while it carries pollen between flowers.Garbage and chemicals pollute a river and harm fish. Cutting trees removes shelter and lets rain wash the soil away. A long drought dries up water.

The water cycle

  • Evaporation: the sun heats liquid water and it changes into water vapor.
  • Condensation: water vapor cools and changes into tiny droplets that form clouds.
  • Precipitation: water falls from the clouds as rain, hail, or snow.
  • Collection: water gathers in rivers, lakes, the sea, and underground, and the cycle starts again.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide

  • During photosynthesis, plants take in carbon dioxide and give off oxygen.
  • Animals take in oxygen and give off carbon dioxide.
  • Plants and animals need each other for the air they use.

Decomposers

  • Fungi and bacteria break down dead plants and animals.
  • They return nutrients to the soil, so new plants can grow.
Remember: matter is used again and again. Water, air, and nutrients move in cycles, and harming one part (like cutting trees or polluting water) affects the living things that depend on it.

Simple machines around us

A simple machine is a tool with few or no moving parts that makes work easier to do. Simple machines do not use electricity or an engine.

MachineWhat it looks likeExamples at home, school, and community
Inclined planeA flat surface that is slantedwheelchair ramp, slide, wooden plank for loading a truck, ramp at the market
WedgeA thin edge that splits or cutskitchen knife, axe, chisel, nail, doorstop
LeverA bar that turns on a fixed pointseesaw, crowbar, claw hammer, bottle opener, scissors
PulleyA wheel with a ropeflagpole, well, construction crane, window blinds

Parts of a lever

  • Fulcrum: the fixed point where the lever turns or rests.
  • Effort: the force you apply to make the lever work.
  • Load: the object the lever lifts or moves.
Tip: use the fulcrum slider on the Home page to see how the effort changes.

Advantages and limitations

MachineWhat it makes easierWhat you give up
Inclined planeA long, gentle ramp needs less effort than lifting straight up.The load travels a longer distance and takes more time. A short, steep ramp is harder to push up.
WedgeA long, thin, sharp wedge splits things with less effort.A thick, blunt wedge needs more effort.
PulleyA single fixed pulley lets you pull down to lift a load up.It changes the direction of the force only. It does not make the load easier to lift.
LeverPushing farther from the fulcrum makes the load easier to lift.You must move through a bigger distance.
Remember: a machine does not make energy and does not do the work by itself. It makes work easier, but you still need to apply effort.

Fair tests with levers

  • A lever can change the size of the force you need and the direction of the force. Pushing down on one end can make the load on the other end go up.
  • Moving the fulcrum closer to the load, or pushing farther from the fulcrum, makes the load easier to lift.

How to make a test fair

StepExample: does moving the fulcrum change how hard it is to lift?
Change only one thingthe position of the fulcrum
Keep everything else the samesame ruler, same load, same person pushing
Observe or measurehow hard or easy it is to lift the load each time
Repeat and recordtry each position more than once and write down what happened
Why only one change? If two things change at once, you cannot tell which one caused the result.

Modern Biotechnology & GMOs

A genetically modified organism (GMO) is an organism whose genes have been changed in a laboratory, often by adding a gene from another organism. Modern biotechnology changes genes directly. Traditional biotechnology (like selective breeding or fermentation) does not.

🌽 Bt cornA gene from a bacterium is inserted so the corn resists pests.
💉 Gene therapyA healthy copy of a gene is placed into a patient’s cells to treat a genetic disorder.
🧪 IVFAn egg and a sperm are joined outside the body, in a laboratory dish.
🐑 CloningProducing a genetically identical copy of an organism.
🌿 Tissue cultureGrowing new plants or cells from a small piece in a lab.
🐄 Selective breedingTraditional method: breeding chosen parents over many generations.
Modern vs. traditional
  • Modern: changes or moves genes directly (genetic engineering, gene therapy).
  • Traditional: uses living things without direct gene changes (fermentation, selective breeding).
💡 A product is modern biotech when a gene is inserted, edited, or replaced, such as a bacterium’s gene in corn.

Weighing Benefits and Risks

Biotechnology brings benefits (higher yields, pest resistance, new medicines, drought tolerance) and concerns (safety, environmental effects, ownership and cost, and whether benefits reach only those who can afford them).

Spot the extreme claims
  • “All GM crops are harmful, ban them”: too extreme. Safety should be tested case by case.
  • “GM crops have no risks, so no testing”: also too extreme.
  • “Biotechnology has only benefits”: No. It has benefits and risks that society must weigh, including ethical and environmental ones.
How to take a position (debate or essay)

State your position clearly. Give one benefit and one concern you considered. Explain why that weighs the way it does.

Evidence of Global Warming

Climate is the long-term pattern of weather. Climate change is a long-term change in the average weather patterns of a place or the whole Earth. Global warming is the rise in Earth’s average temperature.

Evidence that points to warming
  • Rising average global temperature, the most direct evidence.
  • Melting glaciers and polar ice, because ice melts when average temperatures rise.
  • Shrinking Arctic sea ice.
  • Rising sea level, caused by warming seawater expanding and land ice melting into the ocean.
💡 Falling sea levels, growing ice sheets, or thicker glaciers would point the opposite way.
Reading a data table

Look at how the two columns move. If temperature goes up each year while sea-ice or glacier area goes down, the conclusion is: as temperature rises, ice decreases. Don’t pick “not related” or “ice increases” when the numbers clearly say otherwise.

The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is the natural process in which gases in the atmosphere trap heat and keep Earth warm enough for life. Human activity adds more greenhouse gases, strengthening the effect (the enhanced greenhouse effect).

💨 Carbon dioxide (CO₂)Burning fossil fuels (coal, gasoline, oil). Trees take it in.
🐄 Methane (CH₄)Decomposing waste in landfills, livestock, rice paddies.
🌾 Nitrous oxide (N₂O)Nitrogen fertilizer use.
What happens when CO₂ rises?

Earth’s surface gives off heat. More greenhouse gas means more of that heat is absorbed and sent back toward the surface, so the lower atmosphere warms. Burning fossil fuels increases the amount of CO₂ in the air.

💡 Nitrogen, oxygen, and argon make up most of air, but they are not the greenhouse gases.

El Niño & La Niña

El Niño and La Niña are two phases of a natural Pacific Ocean climate cycle (ENSO) that change weather in the Philippines.

🔥 El NiñoWarm phase: surface waters of the central and eastern Pacific become warmer than normal. The Philippines gets below-normal rainfall: drought and dry spells.
❄️ La NiñaCool phase: those surface waters become cooler than normal. The Philippines gets above-normal rainfall: floods and landslides.
Preparing a community
  • Before El Niño: monitor PAGASA advisories, store water early, and shift to drought-tolerant crops before the dry season.
  • Before La Niña: clear drainage canals, secure harvests, and prepare for floods and landslides.
💡 The best plan is early and based on forecasts, not waiting until rivers dry up.

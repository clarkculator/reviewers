How Populations Grow and Stop Growing

A population is all the individuals of one species in an area. It grows when births beat deaths. But no habitat has endless food, water, and space, so growth eventually meets a ceiling.

Carrying capacity (K) is the largest population an environment can support over time, given its food, water, shelter, and space.

What happens as a population nears K, or goes over it?
📈 population climbs➜🍃 resources get scarce➜☠️ death rate rises, birth rate falls➜⚖️ population levels off near K
  • Over K, deaths beat births, so the population shrinks back. The environment’s K does not rise to match the crowd.
  • Overshoot: a population shoots far above K, uses up its resources, and then crashes, sometimes below where it began.
  • Near K, births and deaths are about equal, so growth is close to zero.
💡 Ask: what is the habitat running out of, and what does that do to births and deaths?
K is not a fixed number

K rises or falls when the environment changes. A drought means less water and less plant food, so K drops. A herd that was fine last year may now be over the new K and decline. Plentiful rain can raise K.

💡 When a claim says “K never changes” or “the same herd will be fine,” be suspicious. Always check what changed in the environment.
Limiting factors: density-dependent vs. density-independent
Density-dependentEffect gets stronger as the population gets crowded: predation, disease, parasites, competition for food, space, or mates.
Density-independentHits the population the same way no matter how big it is: floods, wildfires, droughts, typhoons, volcanic eruptions.

Test: would the factor care how many individuals there are? A flood doesn’t. A disease spreads faster when animals are packed together.

Very small populations can decline too (Allee effect)

Growth isn’t always better with fewer individuals. When a population is tiny and spread out, individuals may fail to find mates or cooperate, so births drop and the population can shrink even though there’s plenty of food. This is the Allee effect.

The Logistic Model

The logistic equation gives the growth rate (how many individuals are added per time step):

dN/dt = rN × (K − N) / K

  • r: the population’s natural growth rate when there’s plenty of room.
  • N: current population size.
  • (K − N)/K: the “room left” brake. Near 0 when crowded, near 1 when roomy.
💡 Do it in steps: work out (K − N), divide by K, then multiply by r and N.

Worked example

r = 0.3, K = 600, N = 200. Room left = (600 − 200) / 600 = 0.67. Then dN/dt = 0.3 × 200 × 0.67 ≈ 40 individuals per step.

🎛️ Try the formula

Why can two different population sizes have the same growth rate?

Growth depends on two things pulling in opposite directions: how many parents there are (N) and how much room is left (K − N). Small N means few parents but lots of room. Large N means many parents but little room. These can balance out. Growth is fastest in the middle, around K/2, and slows on both sides.

💡 Try r = 0.5, K = 1000 in the box above with N = 200, then N = 800. Then try N = 500.
Phases of the S-shaped curve
  • Slow start: few individuals, so few births.
  • Rapid (exponential) phase: growth is fastest, the steep middle of the curve.
  • Slowing down: crowding raises deaths and lowers births.
  • Leveling off: births ≈ deaths at K.

What Is Biotechnology?

Biotechnology is using living things, or their parts and genetic materials, to make products or solve problems. It is much bigger than “genetic engineering.”

🍶 TraditionalUsed for centuries, no gene editing or fancy lab needed. Microbes do the work: fermentation (suka, tapuy, yogurt, cheese, nata de coco, toyo) and selective breeding.
🔬 ModernUses lab tools to change or use genes directly: genetic engineering, GMOs, gene editing, DNA technology.
Fixing common misconceptions
  • “Biotech = GMOs only”: No. Traditional fermentation counts, because living microbes make a useful product.
  • “It only happens in laboratories”: No. People have done it in kitchens and barns for centuries.
  • “Fermentation is controlled rotting, so it’s unsafe”: No. It uses helpful microbes under controlled conditions that make food safe, tasty, and longer-lasting.
  • “Microbes are always harmful”: No. Many are helpful.
💡 When you read “Correct, because…” or “Incorrect, because…”, check both halves: is the verdict right, and is the reason true?

Ferment Kitchen

Fermentation is when microbes (bacteria, yeast, or mold) change sugars or other food materials into new things like acids, gases, or alcohol. Some need no oxygen (anaerobic). Others need air (aerobic).

🥥 Nata de coco
  • Ingredients: coconut water, sugar, a little acid (like vinegar), and nata-making bacteria (Acetobacter / Komagataeibacter).
  • These bacteria need oxygen, so the gel grows at the top, where the liquid meets the air.
  • Cover the tray loosely (so air gets in), keep it at room temperature, leave it undisturbed for about 10 to 14 days, and use clean tools.
  • Not sealed airtight, not refrigerated, and no yeast instead of the bacteria.
🍾 Suka (vinegar): two stages
🍬 sugar➜ yeast ➜🍷 ethanol➜ acetic acid bacteria + air ➜🍶 acetic acid (vinegar)

Stage 1 is alcoholic fermentation (no oxygen, yeast). Stage 2 needs oxygen and acetic acid bacteria. A jelly-like film on top (the “mother”) is normal. Fuzzy mold and a rotten smell mean spoilage.

🫘 Toyo (soy sauce)

A koji mold (Aspergillus) grows on soybeans and wheat, then the mix is soaked in salty brine and aged for months. Real fermented labels list soybeans, wheat, salt, and water plus time. Shortcut labels (“ready in one day,” “hydrolysed vegetable protein,” added colourings and flavour enhancers) point to a non-fermented product.

🍚 Tapuy, 🥥 lambanog, 🧀 cheese
  • Tapuy: rice wine made when yeast and mold in a starter ferment cooked rice.
  • Lambanog: coconut sap ferments (yeast turns sugar to alcohol), then it’s distilled to concentrate the alcohol.
  • Cheese: starter bacteria go into warm milk, then rennet is added so the milk proteins clump into curds.
  • All yeast alcohol-making in a closed jar is alcoholic (anaerobic) fermentation.

Who’s Who in an Ecosystem

An ecosystem is a community of living things and the nonliving things around them, all working together. Everyone has a job, and the job is about how you get your energy.

🌱 ProducersMake their own food using sunlight, water, and air (photosynthesis). Every food chain begins with them.
🐛 ConsumersCan’t make food, so they eat other living things. Herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, omnivores eat both.
🦅 ScavengersEat animals that are already dead, usually ones they did not kill. They are still animals that find and eat food.
🍄 DecomposersBreak dead plants and animals down into tiny bits and return nutrients to the soil, so producers can use them again.
Why do plants need light?

Light is the energy source for photosynthesis. Plants capture it and turn water and carbon dioxide into sugar, which is their food. No light means no food made, and when plants struggle, everything that eats them struggles too.

💡 Plants don’t need light to look pretty or to feel warm. They need it to make food.
Scavenger or decomposer? Spot the difference
  • A scavenger is usually an animal you can see taking bites of a carcass.
  • A decomposer works on a much smaller scale, breaking things down until they become nutrients again.
  • Scavengers eat dead things and leave leftovers. Decomposers finish the job and recycle.
💡 Ask: does it just eat the dead animal, or does it turn it back into nutrients for the soil?

Food Chains & Levels

A food chain shows who eats whom. The arrow means “gives energy to”, so it points toward the eater.

🌾 Grass➜🐛 Grasshopper➜🐸 Frog➜🐍 Snake

Count the levels from the producer. The first consumer to eat the producer is primary. The next is secondary, then tertiary.

💡 Trick: put the word “first, second, third” on each eater in order. The producer doesn’t get a number. It’s the starter.
Can an animal be on more than one level?

Yes! In real ecosystems chains overlap into a food web. An animal that eats both plants and animals can be a primary consumer in one chain and a secondary consumer in another. In a single chain, though, each organism holds one place.

Where do decomposers fit?

They work with every level. When any producer or consumer dies, decomposers recycle its nutrients back into the soil. That’s why a chain is really a circle over time.

Living & Nonliving Parts

🐞 BioticLiving things, and things that were once living: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, and also dead leaves, logs, and bones.
🪨 AbioticNever alive: sunlight, water, air, temperature, rocks, and soil minerals.
Why are dead leaves still biotic?

Biotic means “from life.” Something that was once part of a living organism still counts, because it came from life and decomposers will use it. Rocks and sunlight were never alive, so they are abiotic.

💡 Ask: was this ever alive, or made by something alive? If yes, it’s biotic.
Soil: why not all soil is equal
  • Sandy soil: big grains, water drains fast, few nutrients.
  • Clay soil: tiny grains, holds lots of water, can get hard and sticky.
  • Loam soil: a rich mix of sand, clay, and decayed matter. It holds the right amount of water and nutrients, so it’s the best for growing crops.

Notice how a mix of soil (abiotic) and decayed matter (biotic) makes the best home for plants. The two sides depend on each other.

What happens when an abiotic factor changes?

Living things depend on abiotic factors, so a change ripples through the whole community. Think of a small stream that shrinks in dry weather:

💧 less water➜🌱 plants wilt➜🐛 less food for plant-eaters➜🦊 hunters struggle too

Animals may also move away, crowd into smaller spaces, or compete harder for what is left.

Balance & Competition

Predators and prey

A predator hunts and eats another animal, its prey. The two populations rise and fall together, like a seesaw with a delay: more prey means more food, so predators increase. Then more predators eat more prey, and prey drops.

💡 When food runs short, hungry populations shrink. When a predator disappears, its prey can grow too fast and eat too much of what they depend on.

Competition

Living things compete when they need the same limited resource: food, water, light, space, or mates.

  • Same species competing → intraspecific. (Two deer fighting over one patch of grass.)
  • Different species competing → interspecific. (A deer and a rabbit both wanting that grass.)

Competition is not hunting, and it’s not a partnership. Nobody has to eat anybody.

Using evidence to reach a conclusion

Some questions give you a real story and ask what it proves. Good scientists pick the conclusion that the story actually supports, nothing more. Watch out for words like “always” and “never”. One story rarely proves that. Ask: what changed, what happened next, and which choice explains it without stretching?

Living Together (Symbiosis)

Symbiosis means two different species living in a close, long-term relationship. There are three kinds. Decide by asking: who gains, who loses?

💚 MutualismBoth benefit.
Bee gets nectar, flower gets pollinated.
💛 CommensalismOne benefits, the other is not affected (not helped, not harmed).
A bird nests in a tree.
💔 ParasitismOne benefits, the other is harmed.
A tick feeding on a dog.
Parasitism or predation? Both involve “harm.”

A predator kills and eats its prey quickly. A parasite usually lives on or inside its host and takes from it over time, often without killing it right away. Symbiosis is about a close, living-together relationship.

Symbiosis vs. competition: not the same

Symbiosis is between different species who live together closely. Competition is about fighting over the same resource, and it can happen within a species or between species.

What happens when a pond’s water level drops a lot?

Water is an abiotic factor that every pond resident relies on, so a big drop ripples through the whole community.

💧 water shrinks➜🌿 plants dry out or lose light and nutrients➜🐌 plant-eaters lose food and shelter➜🦢 animals that eat them lose food
  • Animals that can move (frogs, birds, insects) may leave.
  • Animals that can’t (fish, snails) get crowded into less space and compete harder for food and oxygen.
  • Warmer, shallower water holds less oxygen, which is harder on animals that breathe it.
💡 Good explanations follow the chain: change in a nonliving factor → producers → consumers → everyone else.

Tricky Pairs

These ideas are easy to mix up. Read each pair, then test yourself.

Scavenger vs. DecomposerScavenger: an animal that eats dead animals it didn’t kill. Decomposer: tiny recyclers that break dead matter into nutrients.
Biotic vs. AbioticBiotic includes once-living things (dead leaves, logs). Abiotic was never alive (water, rocks, sunlight, soil minerals).
Same-species vs. other-speciesFighting over a resource inside one species is same-species competition. Between different species it’s competition too, just between species.
Competition vs. SymbiosisCompetition is about a shared limited resource. Symbiosis is two species living closely together.
Commensalism vs. MutualismMutualism: both gain. Commensalism: one gains and the other is unaffected: no help, no harm.
Commensalism vs. ParasitismCheck the second species. Unaffected means commensalism. Harmed means parasitism.

Think Like a Scientist

The fair test

To find out whether one thing makes a difference, change only that one thing. Keep everything else the same. If two things change at once, you can’t tell which one caused the result.

  • Changed variable: the thing you test.
  • Controlled variables: everything you keep the same.
  • Measured result: what you observe.
